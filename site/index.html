Abstract

Vision-language-action (VLA) models connect visual observations and natural-language instructions to robot actions. Deploying these models on embedded mobile robots requires balancing prediction quality with inference latency and accumulated closed-loop errors. QCarVLA investigates this trade-off on the QCar2 platform through efficient four-camera environment encoding, language-conditioned flow-matching trajectory prediction, and training-time discrete motion supervision. A common benchmark spans the Quanser QLabs digital twin and physical QCar2 hardware, with ordered pickup, stop, and drop-off tasks. Evaluation considers task completion, traffic-rule compliance, drivable-area compliance, recovery dependence, and computational performance on the onboard NVIDIA AGX Orin.

Method

QCarVLA adapts OpenDriveVLA to the sensing geometry and computational constraints of QCar2. A shared FastViT backbone processes four surrounding camera views, supplying scene tokens and BEVFormer map context. The bird’s-eye-view representation covers ±5 m around the vehicle on a 200 × 200 grid.

A conditional flow-matching decoder combines environment, ego-state, and language context to predict six waypoints over a three-second horizon. Four integration steps refine the trajectory, and an NMPC controller tracks it while the policy predicts asynchronously. Feature distillation and auxiliary discrete motion supervision support training.

Benchmark

The benchmark contains 19 routes: 13 for training and six held out for evaluation. Two traffic configurations are used for demonstration collection, with a third held out for closed-loop testing. The same task protocol is used in simulation and on physical hardware.

Each rollout begins at node 10, visits pickup, stop, and drop-off regions in order, and returns to the starting region. The vehicle must stop for three continuous seconds at each task region. The server records at 10 Hz, with a two-minute timeout.

Evaluation measures task completion, stop-sign and traffic-light compliance, off-road time, cross-track error, recovery interventions, and onboard computation. A shared recovery policy assists major deviations; intervention counts are reported alongside route completion.
